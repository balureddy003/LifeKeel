# LifeKeel usability test kit

Date: 2026-11-07. For a researcher with no engineering background. Everything here can be run with a phone, a laptop, a screen recorder and this document.

Read sections 1 to 3 before you recruit anyone. Section 11 (ethics) is mandatory for the stressful-moment group and a good habit for everyone.

Contents: 1 Goals and metrics, 2 Participant groups, 3 Screener, 4 Test environment, 5 Task scripts, 6 Moderator guide, 7 Consent form, 8 Scoring sheet, 9 Device and assistive-tech matrix, 10 Remote unmoderated sessions, 11 Ethics for sensitive sessions, 12 Findings into the loop.

## 0. What LifeKeel is (so you can explain it in one breath)

LifeKeel is a calm record for a UK family. It keeps what is on file (policies, contacts, key papers), answers one money question with one number that both adults confirm, lists the first steps if something happens to one of them, and shows who is covered by which policy. It states facts, dates and steps. It does not recommend products or providers, and you must not either.

Say to participants: "This is a tool that helps a family keep track of what is on file. We are testing the tool, not you. Nothing you do can be wrong."

## 1. Goals and success metrics

### Goals

1. Find where a first-time adult gets stuck between signup and a first useful result.
2. Find whether the second adult (invited partner) can join, get approved and confirm without help.
3. Find out whether the wording feels calm and neutral, not pushy, not like advice.
4. Find barriers for people with access needs, low confidence, or reading difficulty.
5. Find out what people expect to happen at each step (their mental model), including what they expect to be able to say or snap instead of typing.

### Metrics per task

| Metric | How to record | Target to call a task "healthy" |
|---|---|---|
| Task completion | Pass, Pass with help, Fail (see scoring sheet) | 80% unaided Pass across a group of 5 |
| Time on task | Stopwatch from "go" to the participant saying "done" or you stopping | Within 2x the time a team member takes |
| Wrong turns | Count each time they open a page or tap a control that is not on the path | 2 or fewer per task |
| Help requests | Count each time they ask you or say they are stuck | 0 or 1 |
| SEQ (Single Ease Question) | After each task: "Overall, how easy or difficult was that? 1 very difficult to 7 very easy" | Average 5.5 or higher |
| SUS (System Usability Scale) | After the whole session, 10 statements, 1-5 scale (see 8.3) | 68 or higher is average; below 60 needs action |
| What did you expect | Ask at every pause: "What did you expect to happen when you tapped that?" Write the words verbatim | Qualitative: collect, do not score |
| Confidence | "How sure are you it worked?" 1-5 | Average 4 or higher |

Small groups: 5 people per group finds most major problems. Do not report percentages as if they were survey results. Say "4 of 5".

### Trust and tone checks (ask after the first money question and the first-steps task)

- "In your own words, what did that just tell you?" (checks they read it as information, not advice)
- "Did it tell you to do anything? What?" (a "yes, buy / switch / call X" answer is a serious finding, see section 12)
- "How did that feel? Calm, pushy, scary, other?"

## 2. Participant groups

Run 5 people per group where you can; 3 is acceptable for the hardest-to-reach groups on a first round. Participants do not have to be a good fit for LifeKeel's market. They need to be a good fit for the barrier.

| Group | Who | What we want to learn | Session notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Older adults, low smartphone confidence | 65+, uses a phone for calls and messages, not for forms or banking | Can they sign up, read the answer, and find first steps; is text size and wording enough | Use their own phone if possible. Allow 75 minutes. Do not rush. Do not say "just tap" |
| B. Screen-reader users | Daily VoiceOver (iPhone), TalkBack (Android), or NVDA/JAWS (desktop) | Order of reading, labels, focus after actions, the confirm and remove steps | Use their own device and settings. Do not run tasks on a borrowed device. Allow 90 minutes |
| B2. Low-vision | Uses magnification, large text, high contrast, or dark mode | Layout at 200 to 400% zoom, contrast, touch targets | Their own setup |
| C. Dyslexia | Self-reported dyslexia | Reading load, wording, the simple-language mode | Offer to read aloud if asked. Do not time them strictly |
| D. Non-native English | English is not their first language, reads UK English with effort | Plain-language clarity, terms like "on file", "holder", "renewal" | Ask them to explain terms back in their words |
| E. A couple, one partner reluctant | Two adults in a household; one keen, one doubtful or avoiding it | Invite wording, the join and approval flow, whether the reluctant partner feels pressured | Run each partner separately first, then optionally together. The reluctant partner can decline any step at no cost |
| F. Stressful moment | Recent bereavement (roughly 3 to 18 months) or separation, who have opted in knowingly | Whether first steps and wording are bearable, kind and useful | Special care: section 11. Fictional data only. Trained facilitator only |
| G. Control group | General adults 30 to 55, comfortable with phones | Baseline times and wrong turns to compare the others against | Standard session |

People can belong to two groups. Record both.

## 3. Recruiting screener

Use this by phone, email or form. Do not tell people the "right" answers. Read each question as written.

### 3.1 Opening script

"Thanks for your interest. I am running a short research session on a website that helps families keep track of what is on file, such as insurance and key contacts. It takes about 60 minutes, and you will receive [incentive]. I would like to ask a few questions to see if you are a good fit. Your answers are private and used only for choosing participants. You can stop at any time. Is now a good time?"

### 3.2 Questions

Record the answers in the recruiting spreadsheet, not in this document.

1. What is your age band? 18-29 / 30-44 / 45-64 / 65-74 / 75+
2. Do you live in the UK? Yes / No (No: thank and close.)
3. Which devices do you use at home? Phone / tablet / laptop / desktop. Which do you use most?
4. How confident are you using a phone for things like online forms or banking? Very / Fairly / Not very / Not at all
5. Do you use any of these to use a phone or computer? Screen reader (name it) / magnification or large text / high-contrast or dark mode / voice control / none
6. Do you find reading long text difficult, for example because of dyslexia or another reading difficulty? Yes / No / Prefer not to say
7. Is English your first language? Yes / No. If no: how comfortable are you reading English? Very / Fairly / With effort
8. Do you live with a partner or spouse? Yes / No. If yes: would they also take part? Is one of you more interested than the other in organising family paperwork? (Ask neutrally; this is not a pressure question.)
9. Have you looked after family paperwork such as insurance, bills or contacts in the last year? Yes / No
10. Has a close family member died, or has your relationship ended, in the last 18 months? Yes / No / Prefer not to say. Do not ask for details.
    - If yes, read the sensitive-group note in 3.3 before going on.
11. Have you taken part in research or worked in design, technology, insurance, or finance advice? (Exclude if worked in LifeKeel or a competitor.)
12. Can you take part in a session of about 60 to 90 minutes, at [times]? In person / video call / unmoderated?

### 3.3 Sensitive-group note (question 10)

Never recruit someone into group F without a separate, explicit opt-in. Read this aloud:

"The session includes topics like what a family needs to do if something happens to someone. This can feel sad or hard. Everything we use will be made-up data, never yours. You can skip any task, pause, or stop at any time, without giving a reason, and you will still receive the full thank-you. Would you like to take part knowing that?"

If they hesitate, thank them and do not follow up more than once. Do not use group F participants from the same support community on the same day. Do not recruit anyone who says they are in the first weeks of loss or in an unsafe situation (separation with safety concerns).

### 3.4 Selection quotas for a first round

| Group | Target | Backup |
|---|---|---|
| A | 4 | 1 |
| B and B2 | 5 (at least 3 screen-reader, 2 low-vision) | 1 |
| C | 4 | 1 |
| D | 4 | 1 |
| E | 3 couples (6 people) | 1 |
| F | 3 (only with trained facilitator) | 1 |

Over-recruit by one per group to cover no-shows. Pay incentives to everyone who starts, even if they stop. Do not make incentive depend on finishing.

## 4. Test environment

### 4.1 Rules

1. Test data is fictional. Always. Never ask a participant to use their real email, real policy numbers, real registrations, real names of relatives or real documents.
2. Use the staging or test site your team gives you, never the live production site. Ask the team for the address; do not guess one.
3. Each participant gets a fresh fictional persona card (4.2) and a throwaway email address from the team. The team supplies the inbox. You will need two throwaway inboxes for couples (one per partner).
4. Never type or show real data on screen while recording.
5. Ask the team to confirm before every session day: sign-up works, emails arrive (the invite, the approval, the verification), and the test inbox is accessible. If emails do not arrive, stop and reschedule. Do not make a participant wait on a broken test.

### 4.2 Persona card (hand to the participant; make 2 versions for couples)

Fill in names that are clearly made up and not common in the participant's circle.

```
You are: Sam Rowan (adult 1) / Alex Rowan (adult 2, partner)
Household: Sam, Alex, one child, Ivy (age 6)
Email for Sam: [throwaway 1]     Email for Alex: [throwaway 2]
Home: terraced house, mortgage with a bank (made up)
Cover on file: a life policy for Sam with "Northfield Cover" (made up provider),
  renews 14 March; a car policy for Alex with "Harbour Mutual" (made up), renews 2 June
Car: registration "LK21 ABC", Ford Fiesta, MOT due 9 May
A made-up policy letter is provided as a printed page or image on the table or in the participant's gallery.
```

Do not use real provider names. Do not give a person any reason to enter details of a real policy.

## 5. Task scripts

Ten tasks. Each has: what the participant reads, success criteria, where people usually go wrong (watch for it, never hint), and what to record. Give the task text on a card (or in chat for remote sessions). Read it aloud for screen-reader and dyslexia groups if they prefer. Do not read the "success" or "watch for" lines aloud.

Keep tasks in order for groups A, C, D, F and G. For E (couples) run tasks 1 to 7 in order. For B and B2 skip task 8 if time is short.

The product may differ slightly on staging. If a route or label differs, mark it in the notes and carry on.

### Task 1. Sign up

Read aloud: "You have heard about this tool for family paperwork. You are Sam Rowan. Create an account with the email on your card and get as far as the first screen after signing up."

Success: account created; participant reaches the first signed-in screen (the three ways in or the welcome flow).

Watch for: hesitating on email versus password; missing the verification step; confusion about what the carousel or welcome screens ask; a password rule that is not visible before submit; any "why do you need this?" question (record the exact words).

Record: time, wrong turns, SEQ. Ask: "What did you expect to see after you pressed the button?"

### Task 2. First money question

Read aloud: "Imagine you want to understand where your family stands if one of you could not work. Use the tool to get one answer to a money question about your household."

Success: participant starts a brief (Answer a money question), enters the fictional household facts from the card, and reaches a single answer. They can say in their own words what it says.

Watch for: choosing the wrong door; not knowing which figures to enter; treating the answer as a recommendation ("so I should buy more?"); skipping the confirm step; difficulty reading the number. Ask the trust-and-tone questions from section 1.

Record: time, wrong turns, SEQ, plain-words summary. Mark "advice read" Yes/No.

### Task 3. Invite the other adult

Read aloud: "You would like Alex, your partner, to see the same answer. Invite Alex to share this household's information."

Success: an invite is sent to the throwaway inbox for Alex (or a code or link created, depending on what the product offers).

Watch for: not finding where to invite; unsure what Alex will see; fear of "sending the wrong thing"; confusion between a link and an emailed invite; unsure whether the invite has been sent. Record how the participant expects Alex to be told.

Record: time, wrong turns, SEQ. Ask: "What do you think Alex will see when they get it? What will they be able to do?"

### Task 4. Other adult joins, gets the approval email and verifies

For couples, this is Alex's own task, done by the partner on their own device. For single participants, the researcher plays Alex's inbox for the part the participant cannot do.

Read aloud (to Alex): "You received an invitation from Sam. Join the household. If it asks you to wait for approval or to check your email, do that, and finish so that you can see the shared household."

Success: Alex opens the invite, asks to join, gets a clear waiting message, Sam (or the organizer) approves, Alex receives the approval email and completes verification or sign-in, and then sees the shared household.

Watch for: whether the waiting screen says what will happen and for how long; whether Alex knows the approval comes from Sam; trying to sign in before approval (it will fail, and the message must be kind); missing or delayed emails; the email landing in spam; whether the join request can expire (check with the team and note it); reluctance ("do I have to?"). A reluctant partner may stop here. Record the reason, in their words, and do not persuade.

Record: time, wrong turns, SEQ, where they waited, what they expected. Ask Alex: "What did you think was going to happen after you pressed Join?"

### Task 5. Both confirm a "who is covered" list

Read aloud: "Find the list that shows who is covered by which policy. Check it looks right for your household. Both of you should say you have seen it."

Success: participant opens the pack (via the small link under the three ways in, the navigation entry, or the command palette); sees who is covered by which policy; confirms. Then the other adult confirms the same list on their own device. The status shows both have confirmed.

Watch for: finding the list at all (it is a small link, not a big card); reading the matrix with a screen reader; understanding "confirmed" versus "saved"; the second adult being unable to add or edit (a read-only account) and not understanding why; what the participant thinks "both adults confirm" means and whether it feels like a legal act (it is not; record any such belief verbatim).

Record: time, wrong turns, SEQ, confidence that "both have seen it".

### Task 6. See first steps if something happens

Read aloud: "Imagine something happened to someone in the household. Find the short list for the first hours and read it to me."

Success: participant opens the first-steps page (See first steps if something happens) and reads the list. They can say what the first one or two steps are.

Watch for: expectation of advice or arrangements ("will it call the insurer?"); distress or relief; whether the steps feel specific to their household (using what is on file) or generic; whether providers are contacted by the person, not the tool (the copy should say so). Group F: see section 11 before running this task, and offer to skip.

Record: time, wrong turns, SEQ, emotional response in their words. Ask: "Is there anything on that list you would not want to see? Anything missing?"

### Task 7. Add a car

Read aloud: "Your family has a car. Add it, using the details on your card: the registration, make and model, and the date its MOT is due."

Success: vehicle record saved with correct fields. They can find it in the list afterward.

Watch for: where to add (Car and transport pack); date input difficulty (UK date format, calendar versus typing); the meaning of "recovery contact"; whether they expect to scan a document or type; confusion when a field is optional.

Record: time, wrong turns, SEQ. Ask: "How would you have preferred to give the tool this information?" (Typing, photo, saying it aloud, forwarding a letter. This feeds the capture inventory; record verbatim.)

### Task 8. Remove a record

Read aloud: "The car has been sold. Remove it from the list."

Success: participant removes the car, sees the confirm step, and understands the result. They can say whether it can be undone.

Watch for: accidental removal; not finding the control; fear of deleting something important; focus lost after removal (screen-reader group); whether the confirm text says what happens; expectation of an undo (the build offers confirm-before-remove, not undo, so record any dismay).

Record: time, wrong turns, SEQ. Ask: "How sure were you that you removed the right one?"

### Task 9. Change notification settings

Read aloud: "You would like to get fewer reminders, and you do not want any message about your family to appear on your phone's lock screen. Find where to change that."

Success: participant finds notification settings and makes a change they can describe. They say what they changed.

Watch for: finding Settings; understanding the list of message kinds; whether a mute applies to them or the whole household (an open product question; record what they assume); lock-screen wording (messages should be generic); quiet hours expectations.

Record: time, wrong turns, SEQ. Ask: "Who do you think this change applies to, just you or everyone?"

### Task 10. The simple-language mode

Read aloud: "The words in the tool feel long to you. Find a way to make the wording simpler."

Success: participant opens Settings and chooses an Experience Mode that has simple language on (the Adult, Child or Elderly mode; Organizer and Parent are full detail). They notice a change in wording.

Watch for: whether "Experience Mode" is a findable name; whether the mode names are alarming ("Elderly", "Child" are labels the participant may reject); whether the mode changes more than the wording (font size, hidden money, hidden advanced items); whether they can change back.

Record: time, wrong turns, SEQ. Ask: "What did you expect it to be called? What do you think changed?"

### Optional task 11 (for the capture research): "Get this paper in"

Read aloud: "Here is a letter about the car policy. Get what matters into the tool, in whatever way feels easiest."

Do not name any method. Record the method chosen: type, photo, upload from files, forward by email, say aloud, other. Ask: "What would you have done if you were on your own, at home, on a phone?" This informs which capture methods to build; see the input-capture inventory in docs/reviews.

Success: participant gets the details into the tool in any available way and reviews what was read before saving.

Watch for: whether the review-before-save step is understood; trust in what was read; reluctance to upload a photo of a real document; fear of the tool "reading all my email".

## 6. Moderator guide

### 6.1 Before the session (15 minutes)

- Read the persona card and your task cards.
- Check the staging address, the throwaway inboxes, screen recording and audio.
- Remove anything real from your screen and notifications.
- Have a glass of water, tissues and a printed stop card ready (see 11) for in-person sessions.

### 6.2 Opening script (read aloud)

"Thank you for coming. I am [name]. I am here to learn how this tool works for people, and I need your honest reactions. We are testing the tool, not you. If something is confusing, that is the tool's fault, and it helps us a lot to hear it. I will give you a few small tasks on a card. Please say out loud what you are thinking as you go. I may not answer questions during a task, because I want to see what happens on your own. That is not rude, it is how the test works. Everything uses made-up data. You can stop, pause or skip any task at any time, without telling me why. May I record the screen and audio? The recording is only used by our team and deleted after [period]."

Then check consent (section 7) and start recording.

### 6.3 During tasks

Think-aloud works for most people. For group B (screen reader) their speech output overlaps with talk. Ask them to speak between actions, and let their screen reader run. For group C and D do not insist on think-aloud; use retrospective probes ("Tell me what you were doing there").

### 6.4 Neutral prompts (use these; nothing else)

| Moment | Say |
|---|---|
| They go quiet | "What are you thinking?" |
| They stop | "What would you do next?" |
| They ask "should I tap this?" | "What would you do if I were not here?" |
| They ask "is this right?" | "What makes you think it might be right or wrong?" |
| After an action | "What did you expect to happen?" |
| Unclear wording | "How would you explain that in your own words?" |
| Emotional response | "Would you like to pause, or carry on?" |
| They apologise | "You are doing fine. This is about the tool." |
| After a task | "Tell me about that." then SEQ |
| End | "Is there anything else you would like to tell me?" |

### 6.5 Do-not-lead rules

1. Never name a button, a menu or a page. Say "get it done", not "tap Settings".
2. Never say "just", "simply", "easily" or "obviously". They imply that struggling is a failing.
3. Never explain what the tool means or does until the task and its questions are done. If they need to continue, say: "I'll note that, and we can talk about it after."
4. Never answer "what does this word mean?". Ask: "What do you think it means?" Record it.
5. Never react to their success or failure with a face or tone. Keep a steady, friendly voice.
6. Never give financial advice, recommend a provider, or say what they should do with their policies. If asked: "I cannot advise on that. I am here to see how the tool works. For advice, a licensed professional can help."
7. Never touch their device or take over the screen. If they cannot continue, mark "Fail, assisted" and give the smallest next-step hint, then record that you did.
8. When they struggle, wait at least 10 seconds before speaking. For screen-reader users, wait at least 30 seconds, because navigation takes longer.
9. Never push a reluctant partner. If they decline, write the reason and move on.
10. Never ask about the real situation behind fictional data ("Is that like your mum's policy?"). If they volunteer it, listen kindly and note nothing identifying.

### 6.6 When to step in

Step in only for: distress (stop and check in), a bug that blocks progress (note it as a Blocker, restore, resume), a safety issue, or at 5 minutes over the task time budget (default 8 minutes). Say: "Let us move to the next one." Mark Fail.

### 6.7 Closing

Ask: "What was the easiest part? The hardest? Would you use this? Who would you not want to see it?" Run the SUS form. Explain the incentive and deletion promise. Thank them. Remind them: "If anything from today stays on your mind, [support contact] is there." (Provide a real support line for groups F, A.)

### 6.8 Note-taker

If you can, have a note-taker (a second person) record: the time of each critical moment, exact quotes, wrong turns, and the screen state. The moderator should not take notes during tasks.

## 7. Consent form text

Use plain language. Offer large print and read-aloud. Give a copy to the participant.

---

**Research session: LifeKeel. Consent form**

**Who is running this**: [name and role], [organisation], [contact email and phone].

**What this is**: We are testing a website that helps families keep track of insurance, contacts and key papers. We are testing the website, not you. Nothing you do can be wrong.

**What will happen**: You will do about [8 to 10] short tasks using made-up information that we give you. A session takes about [60 to 90] minutes. We will ask what you are thinking and how it felt. You will fill in a short rating form at the end.

**What we will record**: Your screen, your voice and your face (video call only if you agree), and our notes. We will not ask you for any real passwords, policy numbers, account details or documents. Please do not use your own.

**Why we record**: To remember accurately what you said and did. Only the research team will see the recordings. We will keep them for [30] days, then delete them. Our report will use short quotes with no name or details that could identify you.

### 8.3 SUS (read each, participant answers 1 strongly disagree to 5 strongly agree)

1. I think that I would like to use this tool frequently.
2. I found the tool unnecessarily complex.
3. I thought the tool was easy to use.
4. I think that I would need the support of a technical person to be able to use this tool.
5. I found the various parts of the tool were well put together.
6. I thought there was too much inconsistency in this tool.
7. I would imagine that most people would learn to use this tool very quickly.
8. I found the tool very cumbersome to use.
9. I felt very confident using the tool.
10. I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with this tool.

Rule: use the participant's own device and settings whenever possible. Never ask a screen-reader user to switch to a new reader.

## 10. Running remote unmoderated sessions

Use this when moderated sessions are not possible (budget, geography), or to add volume. Not suitable for group F, and not for group B on their first round.

### 10.1 Setup

1. Pick a tool that records the participant's screen and voice and lets you set tasks (ask the team which is licensed). Use the participant's own device.
2. Give each participant a one-page brief: what to expect, the made-up persona card, the throwaway email addresses, time, and the stop rule. Read-aloud audio of the brief for A, C and D.
3. Write tasks as short cards, one per page. Put the success question after each: "Were you able to do it? Yes / Partly / No." then SEQ, then one open question: "What did you expect to happen?"
4. Test the whole script yourself with a colleague first. Fix anything that causes confusion; you cannot rescue a participant live.
5. Pilot with two people. Expect to revise.

### 10.2 What to set up for the team

- A way for the participant to reach the throwaway inbox (a shared link or a mailbox viewer). If the product requires email verification, ask the team to provide a test bypass only if it does not change the flow being tested.
- A help channel: a named email address or a chat with a reply within one working day. Say so in the brief.
- Consent text on the first page with a mandatory tick before the first task.
- A visible Stop button on every page: "Stop and still get paid".

### 10.3 Limits and how to handle them

- You cannot ask "why". Ask for a one-sentence "what did you expect" after each task and a final free-text.
- Time is less reliable. Use it as a rough guide only.
- Check at least one video from each participant. Look for: first click, hesitations over 10 seconds, wrong pages.
- Drop-outs: expect 20 to 30% non-completion. Over-recruit.
- Never ask participants to upload anything real.
- For couples, send the partner's brief separately. Do not share one partner's results with the other.

### 10.4 Analysis

Watch recordings at 1.5x speed. Fill the same scoring sheet. Mark any task where you had to guess as "unclear".

## 11. Ethics for sensitive sessions (group F, and a good standard for all)

Who runs these: a facilitator trained in sensitive research, such as someone who has done bereavement or trauma-informed research training, and who can offer a signpost to support. If your team has none, do not run group F. Pause the group instead and tell the product owner.

Before:
- Separate opt-in consent (see 3.3). Do not recruit in the first weeks after loss or where there is risk to someone's safety.
- Offer a chaperone, a friend or relative, to sit in. They may not use the tool for the participant.
- Let the participant pick the time, place and whether to be recorded.
- Give the participant the task list in advance so there are no surprises. Make clear that they can choose which tasks to do.
- Fictional data only. No real names, no real relatives, no real policies. Do not ask them to imagine their own family member; use the made-up persona.
- Prepare support signposts (a bereavement charity or relationship support service; local, current and tested) and print them.

During:
- Agree a stop signal: any of the word "stop", a raised hand, or a printed card they hold up. Stop immediately, without asking why.
- Check in before task 6 (first steps) and offer to skip: "The next task is about what to do when someone dies or something happens. We can skip it. Would you like to do it?"
- Keep the room calm. Allow silence. Do not say "I know how you feel".
- Never probe into their real situation.
- If they become upset: pause the recording, offer water and time, ask whether to continue. A "no" ends the session with full thanks.

After:
- Give the support signposts, regardless of how it went.
- Do a short check-in call or message the next day (only if they agreed in advance).
- Debrief the researcher and note-taker: you may be affected too.
- Handle recordings with extra care: restrict access, delete early, never use clips in public.

Stop the whole study and tell the product owner if: a participant is harmed, a safeguarding concern appears (for example, a participant discloses abuse or risk), or the tool shows a real person's data by mistake.

Ask the product owner before running group E (couples): a reluctant partner should never be put under pressure by the other partner in the room. Use separate rooms or separate calls for the first part.

## 12. Turning findings into the loop

### 12.1 After each session (same day, 20 minutes)

1. Fill the issue log from your notes and recording. One row per problem, facts only. No solutions in the facts column.
2. Tag each issue with the task, the group, and the evidence (timestamp, verbatim).
3. Hand the raw recording to nobody. Share only the issue log and quotes.

### 12.2 After each group (a week)

1. Merge duplicates. Count "how many of N" (for example, 4 of 5).
2. Rate each issue with the severity scale below.
3. Write one plain-language page: what worked, what did not, what surprised you, top 5 issues with evidence.

### 12.3 Severity scale

| Level | Name | Meaning | Examples | What it becomes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | Blocker | Participant cannot complete a core task, or harm could result | Joiner cannot get in and sees no message; screen-reader cannot reach the confirm button; wrong thing removed with no warning; copy reads as a recommendation or names a provider | A PR now. Treat as stop-ship for the affected flow |
| 3 | Major | Completes only with help or after repeated wrong turns | Cannot find the covered list; unclear waiting state; setting applies to everyone but looks personal | A PR in the next round |
| 2 | Moderate | Completes but slowly or with confusion; wording unclear | A term like "holder" not understood by 3 of 5 | A PR in a batch, or a copy change |
| 1 | Minor | Noticed, but no effect on completion | Small visual annoyance | Backlog; fix when nearby |
| 0 | Observation | Not a defect; an idea, expectation or preference | "I would rather photograph the letter" | Research note; feeds product decisions |

Add 1 level when: affects 3 or more of 5 people, affects a group with access needs, appears on first use, or occurs in a safeguarding situation (separation, bereavement, reluctant partner).

### 12.4 What becomes a PR, a decision or a research note

| Type of finding | Goes to |
|---|---|
| A bug (it does not do what it says) | A PR with a test that fails before the fix. Attach evidence: quote, timestamp, task, device |
| Wording or layout confusion with a clear fix | A copy or UI PR. If the wording touches advice, the team runs the advice-check test; do not rewrite the copy to sound like recommendations |
| A flow that works but conflicts with a product rule (autonomous action, advice, or data use) | A product decision, not a PR. Flag to the product owner and do not propose the fix |
| A choice that depends on policy (safeguarding defaults, who can mute, who can edit) | An owner decision. Put it in the loop ledger as "blocked on owner" with the evidence |
| A new capability (voice, photo, forwarding) | A research note and the input-capture inventory (docs/reviews/input-capture-inventory-2026-11-07.md). Not a PR until the product owner picks it |
| Needs more people to be sure | A follow-up round of tests |

### 12.5 Rules for the loop

1. A PR from a finding states: the issue ID, how many of N, the severity, and what test or re-test proves it fixed.
2. A fix is proved by re-running that task with 3 new people from the same group. Do not close a Blocker on the team's own opinion.
3. Never ask for a change that makes the tool act for the family without human approval, or that gives personal advice. If a participant asks for it ("I wish it just bought the cover"), log it as an observation, not a requirement.
4. Never use participant quotes in marketing without fresh written consent.
5. Update the ship loop ledger (docs/reviews/ship-loop-ledger.md) with the round number, PR links and the owner decisions collected. Merging and deploying remain the owner's.

### 12.6 One-page report template

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Who and how many | Groups, N, dates, build tested |
| Headline | 3 sentences |
| Metrics table | Completion, time, wrong turns, SEQ per task; SUS per group |
| Top issues | ID, severity, how many of N, evidence |
| What worked | |
| Owner decisions needed | |
| Next round | Who, what to re-test |
