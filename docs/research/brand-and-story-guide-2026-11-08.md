# LifeKeel brand and story guide

Date: 2026-11-08. Status: proposal for the owner. Nothing here is live except what is already in #436.

**How this was made.** Five simulated expert reviewers each read the landing story brief
(`landing-story-brief-2026-11-08.md`) and worked it from one craft: a screenwriter, a brand
strategist, a film director, a UK copywriter, and a red-team adviser on advertising standards,
safeguarding and inclusion. They are AI-simulated perspectives, not credentialed people. They are
good at finding gaps and disagreeing with each other. They are not sign-off: claims, advertising
rules and the `.si` site need a qualified human before any paid use.

## 2. Where the panel agreed, and where it did not

**All or nearly all agreed**
- **Cut "Leave them a map, not a mystery."** "Leave them" means "after you've gone": death imagery by implication. It breaks our own rule.
- **Do not say "Every adult has seen it."** It is false when someone is excused or has not confirmed yet. Say "asked to confirm". (Fixed in #436.)
- **Stop saying "both of you".** Lone parents, grandparents, siblings and step-families are left out. Say "every adult" or "everyone".
- **The headline "The day someone needs it, it is already there."** Four of five reviewers had a problem with it. See section 3.
- **Drop the big-emotion references.** Apollo 13, Thai Life, John Lewis, Volvo and Pixar's list pull toward heroics and tears. Keep the restraint, not the costume.
- **Show the product plainly**, with its real words ("family list", "look again"), once the feeling has landed.
- **Never put a screen on show that watches a person.** Ticks belong to the list, not to people.

## 3. The headline

You chose "The day someone needs it, it is already there." before the panel reported. The case
against it, from four reviewers working separately:
- **It is a euphemism.** Most readers ask "which day?" and answer "the funeral".
- **"It is already there" promises three things that can each fail:** that the list is complete, up to date, and reachable by the right person. It is only there if someone added it.
- **It lets the whole value hang on a crisis.** The product helps on an ordinary Tuesday.

Candidates that keep the calm and are true:

| Line | From | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Know where to look.** | Copywriter (top pick), consistent with the strategist's essence | Three words, all true, repeatable. My recommendation. |
| A spare key to what your family keeps. | Screenwriter, adapted | Strong image. "Everything" over-claims, so I trimmed it. |
| Everyone can help when everyone can see. | Screenwriter | Warm, inclusive, a little long. |
| What you add today is where your family can find it. | Red-team | Honest. Puts the work with the family. |
| Everything important lives in one person's head. | Strategist (the enemy) | Better as a supporting line than a headline. |

**Recommendation:** headline **Know where to look.** and supporting line "One list for what your
family would look for. Policies, cars, school dates, who to call. Every adult is asked to confirm
they have seen it." The spare key is the image, not the line.

## 4. The story

**Spine.** An ordinary household where one person holds everything. The other adult has to ask,
and asking again feels like being a passenger. The family keeps one list. The other adult looks, nods,
and one day, without asking anyone, knows where to look.

**Three beats**
1. **A Tuesday.** Small things live in one head: an MOT date, a trip deposit, a school number.
2. **The look.** Two people look at the same list. "Oh, you know that too." Every adult is asked to confirm; a change asks them to look again.
3. **The rest.** Someone is away, ill or busy, and the other adult finds it without calling. End on rest: someone sits down, sleeps, or does not have to ask.

**Story rules.** *Always:* the other adult is the hero; small stakes; show the moment someone looks
again; show the product plainly in the last third; end on rest. *Never:* a "someday", "the day" or
"when you're gone" line; hospitals, empty chairs, funerals, sirens or anyone crying; a screen that
watches a person; anyone buying, switching or comparing a policy; any suggestion the organizer did it wrong.

## 4a. The story system: chapters that grow with the product

The product already has far more than the landing page tells. Telling each feature one by one would
turn the page into a list that gets longer every month. Instead, the story has **seven chapters,
each a thing a family says out loud**, and every use case is a **scene** inside one of them. A new
use case never changes the story. It adds a scene.

**What this helps families do, in plain words.** Ask less ("where's the...?"). Make sure nobody is the only one who knows. Let dates stop slipping. Have a calm place to start in a bad week. Stay in control of who sees what. None of this needs a statistic, and none should be quoted without evidence.

### The scene template (how a new use case joins the story)

Every use case is told the same way, so a new one is cheap to add and always sounds like us:

1. **The line a family says out loud**, in their words: "Where's the home insurance?"
2. **The ordinary object**: a policy letter, an MOT date, a school bag, a key.
3. **What the list does about it**, in one plain sentence that maps to one claim.
4. **The real screen**, never an invented one.
5. **One of the cast**, so no new character is needed. Marcia finds the claim packet. Nan finds the school number.
6. **A rest line**: who gets to sit down.

### Rules for adding a use case to the story

- It starts from a question families actually ask, not from our feature name.
- It passes the **Tuesday test**: useful on an ordinary day, not only in a crisis.
- It maps to a live claim with evidence. No claim, no scene.
- It fits one of the seven chapters. If it does not, argue for an eighth before building.
- It passes the eight pre-publication questions in section 9.
- It is told in the shared voice (section 7) and at grade 9 or below.
- The landing page shows **six scenes at most**, led by what is most used. The rest live in a library, "Things your family would look for", and feed the weekly "One Thing Thursday" format.

### How the page and the code carry it

The landing page already has the right mechanism: `components/landing/capabilities.ts` is the story
board, one entry per capability grouped by moment, each tied to a claim, and tests enforce that only
live claims appear. The change is to regroup those moments into the seven chapters, rewrite each line
in the family's words, and add the family list and the confirmation as chapter 1 and 2. A new use case
is then one line in that file and one claim in `claims.ts`.

**One mismatch to fix before this ships.** The registry marks `monthly-letter` ("Monthly letter about
what changed") as planned, but the monthly note exists in the code and sends today. The claim is out of
date, so the page cannot yet say it.

## 5. Characters and animation

You asked for animation and characters. This is a design for them, shaped by the panel. Nothing is built yet.

### The cast

Illustrated, fictional, and always labelled that way. Simple flat shapes and warm skin tones across
the real range of UK households. Children are drawn as simple shapes: no faces, no uniforms, no
school crests, no real-looking names. The same people appear on the sample list in the product so
the story and the screen match.

| Character | Who they are | Their object | Where they appear |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Marcia** | Gran, early 60s, dry humour, reading glasses | The key hook | The main story. She is the other adult. |
| **Dev** | Her son, mid-30s, back at home. He keeps the list. | His phone | The main story |
| **Asha** | Dev's daughter, 9. A shape, not a face. | A school bag | The main story |
| **Priya and Nan** | A lone parent with flu, and the grandparent who steps in | A biro | A short loop |
| **Femi and Kwame** | Siblings sharing the care of a parent, 400 miles apart | A kettle | A short loop |
| **Two homes** | Separated parents with a bag that changes hands | Two key hooks | A short loop |

**The signature character is not a mascot.** The panel advised against giving the in-app assistant a
face, a personality or "I" statements, especially in a bad week. A list does not need a character.
The cast are people, not a brand mascot.

### The main animation: "The Key Hook" (about 45 seconds)

Six beats. Every on-screen word maps to something the product does today.

1. **7:40 a.m.** Toast, steam, a calendar page on the fridge with a date circled.
2. **Dev adds the car.** One thumb tap, and a line appears on the list.
3. **The look.** Two mugs. The phone slides across. Marcia nods and a tick appears: "Marcia has looked."
4. **Weeks later.** Rain. The car will not start. Dev is away: a short text, nothing more.
5. **Marcia opens the list.** The car, its date, and who to call. She is unbothered.
6. **Asha hangs the key on the hook.** Card: **Know where to look.** Free to start.

### Three short loops (10 to 15 seconds each, for social and email)

- **Two hooks:** two doors, two key hooks, one list. "Something changed. Please look again."
- **Nan's turn:** Nan stops herself calling up the stairs, opens the list, writes the MOT on her hand, and lets Priya sleep.
- **The other kettle:** Kwame helps from 400 miles away. Femi sits down for the first time in the film.

### Motion and sound rules

- **Calm motion.** Small things move: steam, a key swinging, a tick appearing, a phone sliding. Nothing zooms, shakes or flashes.
- **Sound is a kitchen.** Kettle, fridge hum, spoon, rain, a chair. Never heartbeats, sirens, ticking clocks, swelling strings or sad piano. One instrument, only after the feeling has landed.
- **On the website:** scroll-triggered, never scroll-jacked. A visible pause control (people must be able to stop motion). Honours reduced-motion by showing still frames with captions. No autoplay audio. Every scene has a text alternative. No flashing. Lazy-loaded, with a small weight budget.
- **The last beat is the real product screen**, not an invented one. No claim appears in motion that is not in the claims registry.
- **Safeguarding.** Never animate one adult checking up on another, never show "who has not confirmed" as an accusation, and never show a screen that tracks where a person is. A tick belongs to the list.

### How it would be built

Inline SVG and CSS with the motion library the site already uses. One new landing component with a
test for reduced motion, the pause control, text alternatives and the claims it relies on. It
would be one PR after you choose the headline and the cast. Marketing video would use the same
characters and shot list, filmed or animated.

## 6. Look and sound (direction to test)

- **Light.** Ordinary British overcast daylight. No golden hour, no lens flare.
- **Palette.** Oat, linen and tea-stained paper as the base. One deep blue-green. One warm ochre for "look here", the colour of a manila folder. Never pillar-box red as a brand colour: red means alarm.
- **Texture and type.** Matte paper, worn wood, fridge enamel. A humanist sans like good public signage, sentence case, tabular figures for dates. No handwriting fonts.
- **Three recurring objects.** The key hook by the door (who can open what, with no hint of surveillance). The fridge door (one place everyone knows to look). Two mugs (the shared pause).
- **Ban list.** The golden-hour hug, the white kitchen with a laughing family of four, a lone figure at a rain-streaked window, the empty chair, hospital corridors, grandparents as props of mortality, the sad piano.
- **A bad-week screen.** The same oat background and the same type: the page does not change mood to signal danger. An ochre bar reading "Here is where to start". The first line, in plain body text, is "In an emergency, call 999." One step per card. No countdowns, no warning triangles.

## 7. Naming and voice

- **LifeKeel** stays the masterbrand. Tell the keel story once, on the About page.
- **"Keel" the assistant** stays a light label with no face and no "I" statements. On a bad-week screen, never write "Keel" as the subject: in UK English, "keeled over" means collapsed.
- **Rename "Keel Plus" to "LifeKeel Plus".** It reads like an assistant upgrade but is photo-reading of documents. Two "Keel"s confuse the paid tier. (Decision for you.)
- **Remove sea words from all user copy** ("sea state", anchors, harbours, storms). Keep them in the code. Storm imagery is a fear metaphor.
- **Say "shared family list", the same way every time.** Not "brief", "roster" or "runway".

**Six do and don't pairs**
- Do say what is on file: "Your car's MOT is due on 3 March." Don't: "Don't get caught out!"
- Do use plain nouns: "your family list". Don't: "your household brief".
- Do say "you can": "You can excuse someone who cannot confirm." Don't: "You should add life cover."
- Do write for any household: "Everyone who shares the list has looked." Don't: "Both of you" as a default.
- Do be dry, not jokey: "One less thing in one person's head." Don't: "Adulting, sorted!"
- Do name the limit: "This is information, not advice." Don't: "We've got you covered."

## 9. Guardrails

**Never say or show**
1. Coffins, funerals, hospital beds, empty chairs.
2. A child crying or looking for a parent.
3. A widow or widower searching drawers.
4. Ticking clocks or countdowns.
5. "Peace of mind", "protect your family", "secure your family's future".
6. "Already there", "always", "everything" or "never miss" without qualification.
7. "Both of you" as the default.
8. Lodgers, carers or helpers using the list (they see everything today).
9. One adult checking whether another has confirmed.
10. Any insurer, policy brand or "better cover".
11. Statistics about death, illness or under-insurance.
12. Testimonials or "families trust us".
13. Lions, NHS, Apollo, Volvo or John Lewis names, looks or logos.
14. Any suggestion it is a will, a lasting power of attorney, legal help or an emergency service.
15. "Income protection is not on file" phrased as a gap to fix.

**Eight questions every campaign must pass**
1. Does every claim map to a live entry in `claims.ts` with evidence?
2. Is it still true if the list is empty, out of date, or the viewer has been removed?
3. Does it still work with the frightening part taken out?
4. Could a lone parent, a multi-faith family or a disabled adult see themselves in it?
5. Could an abusive partner use the feature as shown?
6. Is there no named provider and no "should"?
7. Is it grade 9 or lower, with no jargon?
8. Has a qualified reviewer signed it off, including the `.si` language check, before any paid use?

**Culture and faith.** Some families find open planning for death uncomfortable or private. Some
avoid conventional life insurance. Some bury within a day or two, so "first steps" must never claim
to fit faith practice. Do not treat holding insurance as the sign of a responsible family.

**Safeguarding.** A shared list of schools, addresses and policies could serve an abuser as a map of
the family. Never present confirmation as a way to check up on a partner. Separation answers should
point to Refuge or the National Domestic Abuse Helpline, not to the product. Removing someone cuts
off access but they have already seen the list: never imply removal protects you.

**The `.si` site.** "Call 999" is wrong in Slovenia, where it is 112. Check language and numbers there.
